Blinds for care homes and GP surgeries: what to check

Cords, fire performance, cleaning and privacy: the four things worth checking before anything is ordered.

Blinds in a care home or surgery have more to do than blinds in an office. They hang in rooms where vulnerable people spend time. They are looked at by fire risk assessors and infection control leads. They have to give privacy without making a treatment room feel shut in. Here is what the guidance says on each and how to check what you already have.

Quick answers

The short version, before the detail.

Are looped cords a risk in care settings?

Yes. The Department of Health said so in 2010. A Department of Health alert of 8 July 2010, Window blinds with looped cords or chains, says looped cords and chains can present a strangulation hazard to children and vulnerable adults. It said risk assessments should be carried out on looped blind cords, primarily in healthcare environments where children and vulnerable adults are commonly present. It also said chains at the bottom of vertical blinds should be considered. Children visiting a care home or attending a surgery fall under the same warning.

Do blinds in a care home have to be fire retardant?

They should meet a recognised fire performance standard. The risk assessment decides which. The government guide Fire safety risk assessment: residential care premises says soft furnishings and textiles that ignite easily or spread flame quickly should be avoided where practicable in any type of care home. It says all should meet a recognised fire performance standard. It lists BS 5867-2 for fabrics for curtains and drapes, including roller blinds. It also refers to Type B. Our guide to fire retardancy explains what the types mean.

What about cleaning and infection control?

Blinds belong on the cleaning schedule like everything else in the room. NHS England’s National standards of healthcare cleanliness 2025 set cleaning by functional risk category. A trust quick reference adapted from them says blinds, curtains and screens should be visibly clean and on the cleaning schedule with records kept. They should be cleaned or changed straight away if visibly soiled. Fabric ones should be laundered professionally.

Diagnosis

What to check in each room

Five things you can see on a walk round the building. Photograph anything that gives you pause.

Looped cords or chains in rooms people use

The Department of Health alert is about exactly this. Look at waiting areas, lounges and bedrooms as well as treatment rooms. Anywhere a child visiting or a vulnerable adult could reach a loop counts.

Vertical blinds with a chain at the bottom

The alert says chains at the bottom of vertical blinds should be considered as well. They are easy to overlook because they hang near the floor rather than at the side of the window.

No label, invoice or certificate for the fabric

A fire risk assessor will want to know what the fabric is. A blind with no label and no paperwork is hard to account for. Photograph any label on the back of the blind or at the hem before anyone quotes for anything.

Fabric that cannot be wiped down or laundered

Blinds that collect dust or take a stain and cannot be cleaned are a problem on a cleaning schedule. Treated fabrics also depend on correct laundering to keep their fire performance, so how a blind is cleaned matters twice.

Consulting or treatment rooms where people can see in

A room that overlooks a street or a corridor needs a blind that closes fully when it has to. Dim-out and blackout fabrics and frosted or obscure options all exist. The right one depends on how much light the room needs.

Background

Why it gets overlooked

Each concern has a different owner

Cords tend to land with whoever leads on health and safety. Fire performance goes to the fire risk assessor. Cleaning goes to infection control and privacy to the manager of the room. A blind touches all four and can end up on nobody’s list.

The guidance is in separate documents

The cord warning is one alert. The fire guidance is a separate guide for residential care premises. The cleaning guidance sits in the national cleanliness standards and in the trust documents adapted from them. No single page tells you what to check on a blind.

Fire performance is a matter for the risk assessment

The care premises guide refers to Type B and says all soft furnishings should meet a recognised standard. It does not tell you what your building needs. The risk assessment does that. It also notes that flame-retardant treated textiles depend on correct laundering, so a fabric that was compliant when it was fitted may not be now.

Privacy and cleaning pull in different directions

Heavy fabrics give privacy but are harder to keep clean. Wipe-clean fabrics are easier to look after in a clinical room. Dim-out fabrics reduce light and view and frosted or obscure options keep the view out while letting light in, so the answer often differs from room to room.

What to do about it

What to do about it, cheapest first

Looking is free. Replacement is for blinds that cannot be made safe or clean or accounted for.

1

Walk the building and photograph every blind

Free. One pass catches the cords, the chains, the labels and the fabric. A photo of each blind with its label, headrail and cord arrangement is what it takes to say anything useful about it.

2

Deal with the cords first

Chain tensioners fixed to the wall or frame keep a loop taut. Cleats keep cords out of reach and RoSPA advises one at least 1.5 metres from the floor. Some blinds have breakaway connectors. Where a blind cannot be made safe, a cordless or tensioned replacement is the answer.

3

Put the fabric evidence on file

A label, an invoice or the fabric supplier’s test report goes in with the fire risk assessment. Our guide to fire retardancy explains the types and what an assessor is asking for.

4

Choose fabric that can be kept clean

Wipe-clean fabrics are easier to keep clean in clinical rooms and so are cordless or tensioned blinds with nothing to collect dust at floor level. Where fabric blinds are used they need a cleaning route that fits the schedule.

5

Replace what cannot be made safe, clean or accounted for

In a building that is in use, fitting has to be planned around residents and clinics. It is reasonable to ask any fitter how access and timing would work and to agree it before work is scheduled.

Questions people ask

Blinds in care homes and surgeries, answered properly.

Are looped blind cords a risk in a care home or surgery?

Yes. The Department of Health has said so. A Department of Health alert of 8 July 2010 says looped cords and chains can present a strangulation hazard to children and vulnerable adults. It said risk assessments should be carried out on looped blind cords, primarily in healthcare environments where children and vulnerable adults are commonly present.

It also said chains at the bottom of vertical blinds should be considered. Safe-by-design blinds, meaning cordless blinds or blinds with concealed or tensioned cords, remove the hazard. Our guide to blind cord safety covers the options in more detail.

What fire standard should care home blinds meet?

A recognised fire performance standard. The risk assessment sets which. The government guide to fire safety risk assessment in residential care premises says soft furnishings and textiles that ignite easily or spread flame quickly should be avoided where practicable in any type of care home. It says all should meet a recognised fire performance standard and lists BS 5867-2 for fabrics for curtains and drapes, including roller blinds.

This page does not pick a type for you. That is for the fire risk assessment of your building. Our guide to fire retardancy explains Types A, B and C and what a certificate has to show.

How often should blinds be cleaned?

As often as your cleaning schedule says. Blinds should be on it. NHS England’s national standards of healthcare cleanliness set cleaning by functional risk category. A trust quick reference adapted from them says blinds, curtains and screens should be on the cleaning schedule with records kept. This page does not give frequencies because they depend on the risk category of the area. Visibly soiled blinds should be cleaned or changed straight away.

Which blinds are easiest to keep clean in a clinical room?

Wipe-clean fabrics with cordless or tensioned operation. A fabric that can be wiped down is simpler to keep to a schedule than one that has to come down and be laundered. Cordless or tensioned operation means there is no loop of cord to collect dust or to clean around. Where fabric blinds are used the guidance is for them to be laundered professionally.

What gives privacy in a consulting room without making it dark?

Frosted or obscure options let light in while keeping the view out. Dim-out and blackout fabrics are used where a room needs to be darker or fully private. Frosted and obscure options keep the view in from outside restricted while letting daylight through. The right choice depends on the window and on what the room is used for. It is easier to judge from a photo than from a description.

Can I send a photo and find out?

Yes. It is the right first step. A photo of each blind with its label, headrail and any cords, along with your postcode, tells Simone what she would look at first. It is free and nothing is ordered on the strength of it. If the building is occupied it is worth saying so in the message, because access and timing affect the plan.
