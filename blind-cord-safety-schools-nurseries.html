Nurseries and schools · Blind cord safety

Are the blind cords in our nursery or school safe?

What the rules say about blind cords in childcare settings and what to do about the ones you already have.

A parent asks. An inspector looks round the room. A new member of staff notices a loop of cord hanging beside a cot. The standard for blinds changed in 2014 and the guidance on older blinds is easy to misread. Here is what the rules cover, what they leave to your risk assessment, how to spot a problem from the doorway and the cheapest ways to put it right.

Quick answers

The short version, before the detail.

Is there a law about blind cords?

There is a safety standard for supplying and fitting blinds. It is not a rule about the blinds you already have. On 28 February 2014 BSI published BS EN 13120 for internal blinds along with two companion standards on test methods and safety devices. The British Blind and Shutter Association says in its Make it Safe leaflet that it is now illegal to supply or professionally install an internal blind that does not comply. Trading standards guidance on window blinds puts that duty on anyone who makes, imports, distributes, sells, supplies or fits blinds under general product safety law.

Do we have to replace older blinds?

Not under any blind-specific rule. The risk still has to be dealt with. The rules cover supplying and fitting blinds. There is no blind-specific legal duty to replace older blinds that are already in place. RoSPA advises that looped blinds made before 2014 should be replaced where possible and replaced if safety devices cannot make them safe. It describes this as a duty of care for anyone responsible for others, a landlord for example. A setting looking after children would be sensible to read it the same way.

What will an inspector expect?

The hazards in the room covered by your risk assessment, cords included. The EYFS statutory framework for group and school-based providers does not mention blinds or cords. It does require providers to "take all reasonable steps to ensure staff and children in their care are not exposed to risks and must be able to demonstrate how they are managing risks". An inspector reading your risk assessment will reasonably expect the hazards in each room to be covered. Cords are one of them.

Diagnosis

What to look for in the room

Five things you can check from the doorway with a phone in your hand. Any one of them is worth a photo.

Long looped cords or chains

A cord or chain that hangs in a loop is the hazard the standard is aimed at. The longer the loop the bigger the problem. Cords that pool on a sill or the floor are the worst case.

A chain with no tensioner

A chain tensioner is a small fitting fixed to the wall or window frame that keeps the loop pulled taut. If the chain swings free there is either no tensioner or it has come loose. It is a small part to add.

Cords not on a cleat or on a cleat that is too low

RoSPA advises tying cords up out of reach with a cleat at least 1.5 metres from the floor. A cleat at the height of a toddler’s hand does not do the job. Neither do cords left hanging beside it.

Cots, beds or climbable furniture near the window

RoSPA advises keeping cots, beds, high chairs and any furniture children can climb away from windows. In a nursery this is often a layout question rather than a blind question. It is usually the quickest thing to change.

Blinds that predate 2014 or have no label or instructions

A blind from before the 2014 standard may have no safety device at all. Newer blinds should carry warnings and instructions, so one with neither deserves a closer look. The age is often easier to find from the paperwork or a photo of the headrail than from memory.

Background

Why it is so often misunderstood

The standard is about supplying and fitting

The changes in 2014 cover blinds being supplied and fitted. The British Blind and Shutter Association says a professional installer must fit the safety devices. Blinds that are safe by design meet the standard: cordless blinds, blinds with concealed cords and blinds with tensioned cords or chains.

No blind-specific rule orders older blinds out of a building. That is why "it is not illegal" and "it is not safe" can both be true of the same blind.

Existing blinds are a risk assessment matter

What you already have is dealt with under the general duty to manage risk in the premises. The EYFS framework requires risk assessments to identify what is checked, when and by whom. Someone has to look at the cords, decide whether they are safe and write down what was done. The writing down is the part that is easy to skip.

In a school the duty sits with the employer

Department for Education guidance on health and safety responsibilities and duties for schools says the employer is accountable, with the proprietor in that role at an independent school. Day-to-day tasks are delegated to the headteacher and staff. Who the employer is depends on the type of school: the local authority, an academy trust, a governing body or a proprietor. The guidance does not mention blinds. Cords sit under it like any other hazard in the building.

Children are not the only people at risk

A Department of Health alert from 8 July 2010, Window blinds with looped cords or chains, says looped cords and chains can present a strangulation hazard to children and vulnerable adults. It asked for risk assessments on looped blind cords, primarily in healthcare environments where both groups are commonly present. Our guide to blinds in care homes and surgeries covers that setting.

What to do about it

What to do about it, cheapest first

The first three can often be done without replacing anything. Replacement is the right answer for a blind that cannot be made safe.

1

Tie the cords up out of reach

Free or nearly free. RoSPA advises tying cords up out of reach with a cleat at least 1.5 metres from the floor. It only works if the cord is wound up every time the blind is used, so it suits blinds that are rarely moved better than ones used all day.

2

Fit tensioners or safety devices

A tensioner fixed to the wall or frame keeps a looped cord or chain taut so it cannot form a hazardous loop. Some blinds can be fitted with a safety device after the event and some cannot. It depends on the make and the age, which is what the photo check is for. A blind that cannot be made safe this way goes to the last option.

3

Move cots, beds and climbable furniture

It costs nothing and it changes the risk straight away. It does not remove the cord, so it sits alongside the other options rather than replacing them.

4

Replace with blinds that are safe by design

Cordless blinds, spring-operated blinds, motorised blinds and blinds with concealed or tensioned cords or chains are safe by design. Integral blinds sealed between the glass have no cords at all. This is the answer where an older blind cannot be made safe or where the blinds are due for replacement anyway.

Questions people ask

Blind cord safety, answered properly.

Is it illegal to have looped blind cords in a nursery or school?

No blind-specific rule bans blinds you already have. The risk still has to be managed. The 2014 standards cover supplying and fitting. The British Blind and Shutter Association says it is illegal to supply or professionally install an internal blind that does not comply. There is no blind-specific legal duty to replace older blinds that are already in place.

That does not make an old looped cord acceptable. Settings have to manage the risks in their premises and RoSPA advises replacing looped blinds made before 2014 where possible. This page is not legal advice.

What changed in 2014?

The standard for internal blinds was rewritten around child safety. BSI published BS EN 13120:2009+A1:2014 on the performance and safety of internal blinds on 28 February 2014. It came with BS EN 16433:2014 on test methods for strangulation protection and BS EN 16434:2014 on requirements and tests for safety devices.

The Make it Safe leaflet from the British Blind and Shutter Association lists what changed: blinds must be child safe in all homes whether or not children live there; cord and chain lengths are limited; safety devices must stop a hazardous loop forming; safety devices are fitted at the point of manufacture; safety-critical parts are tested; and warnings and instructions are required.

How serious is the risk?

It kills quickly, which is why it is worth checking. RoSPA says it is aware of at least 33 deaths in the UK from blind cords over the preceding 20 years. The National Child Mortality Database recorded nine children dying from blind cord strangulation between April 2019 and March 2022. RoSPA says a toddler can lose consciousness and die within two to three minutes.

Will an inspector ask about blind cords?

They are not named in the framework. Hazards in the room are expected to be covered. The EYFS statutory framework for group and school-based providers, effective 1 September 2026, does not mention blinds or cords. It requires premises to be fit for purpose and suitable for the age of the children. It also requires providers to "take all reasonable steps to ensure staff and children in their care are not exposed to risks and must be able to demonstrate how they are managing risks".

An inspector looking at the risk assessment will reasonably expect the hazards in the room to be covered. Having the cords checked and the check written down answers the question before it is asked.

Who is responsible for this in a school?

The employer, with day-to-day tasks delegated to the headteacher and staff. Department for Education guidance says the employer is accountable for health and safety. At an independent school that is the proprietor. Who the employer is depends on the type of school: the local authority, an academy trust, a governing body or a proprietor. The guidance does not mention blinds specifically, so cords are covered as a hazard in the building like any other.
